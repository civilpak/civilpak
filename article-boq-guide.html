Understanding BOQ: A Site Engineer's Guide

6 MIN READ · FIG. 01
← Back to Articles

What a BOQ actually is

A Bill of Quantities (BOQ) is a document that breaks a construction project down into measured items — so many cubic metres of concrete, so many square metres of brickwork, so many kilograms of reinforcement — each with a unit rate and a total cost. It's prepared from the drawings and specifications, usually by a quantity surveyor, before tendering begins.

Think of it as the bridge between the drawing and the invoice. The drawing shows what to build. The BOQ tells every contractor bidding on the job exactly how much of it there is, so their prices can be compared on equal terms.

Why it matters on site

For a site engineer, the BOQ isn't just a pricing document — it's a working reference used constantly during construction:

  • It's the basis for interim payment certificates — what you claim each month is measured against BOQ quantities.
  • It flags variations early. If actual site quantities run well past the BOQ figure, that's your signal to raise a variation before the cost overrun becomes a dispute.
  • It protects you in disagreements. A well-kept BOQ with accurate measurement records is the strongest evidence in any claim.

How a typical BOQ is structured

Most BOQs, regardless of the method of measurement used, follow a similar shape:

  • Preliminaries — site setup, supervision, temporary works, insurance, and other costs not tied to a specific trade.
  • Measured works — the bulk of the document, organised by trade or building element: earthwork, concrete, masonry, roofing, finishes, and so on.
  • Provisional sums — allowances for work that can't be fully defined at tender stage (e.g. a PC sum for sanitary fittings still being selected).
  • Prime cost sums — fixed allowances for named subcontractors or suppliers, to which the main contractor adds their own margin.
Site tip Keep a running comparison between BOQ quantities and actual measured-to-date quantities for every major item. The gap tells you where a variation is forming, often weeks before it shows up in a payment dispute.

Hidden costs that get missed

Most BOQ-related disputes come from a handful of recurring blind spots:

  • Wastage allowances that were priced too low for actual site conditions (cutting waste on tiles, offcuts on steel, spillage on concrete).
  • Provisional sums left untracked — treated as "extra" work is agreed but never formally logged against the sum.
  • Verbal variations — instructed on site, never confirmed in writing, and disputed at final account stage.
  • Preliminaries under-priced at tender to win the job, then quietly absorbed as a loss rather than claimed.

A quick checklist before you sign off on a BOQ

  • Do the measured quantities match a re-measure from the latest drawings, not an earlier revision?
  • Are provisional sums clearly marked, with a process for tracking what's spent against each one?
  • Is there a wastage percentage built into brittle or cut-heavy materials (tiles, blockwork, steel)?
  • Are preliminaries realistic for the actual site duration and conditions, not just copied from a template?

A BOQ is only as useful as the discipline behind it. Read it once at tender stage, then keep reading it — against actual site progress — for the rest of the project.
