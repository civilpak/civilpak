Estimation Techniques Every QS Should Know

7 MIN READ · FIG. 02
← Back to Articles

Why the technique you choose matters

Estimation isn't one skill — it's several, used at different points in a project's life. Pricing a concept sketch at feasibility stage needs speed, not precision. Pricing a tender needs precision, not speed. Using the wrong technique for the stage you're at is one of the most common (and costly) mistakes a QS can make.

The three levels of estimation

Most estimating work falls into one of three levels, moving from rough to exact as the design matures:

MethodWhen it's usedHow it works
Approximate / conceptualFeasibility, early budgetingCost per m² of floor area, or per functional unit (per bed, per parking space), based on historical data from similar projects.
Unit rate estimatingTender stage, once a BOQ existsEach measured item priced using a rate — either from a cost database or a first-principles build-up.
Detailed / analyticalTender pricing, cost planningEvery rate broken into labour, material, plant, overheads, and profit — built up from scratch rather than looked up.

Approximate estimating: fast, not precise

At feasibility stage, the drawings barely exist — sometimes it's just a floor area and a building type. The functional unit method (cost per bed for a hospital, cost per parking bay for a car park) or the superficial floor area method (cost per m²) let you produce a workable budget number in hours rather than weeks. The trade-off is accuracy: expect a margin of error of 15–25% at this stage, and say so explicitly when you present the number.

Unit rate estimating: the tender workhorse

Once a BOQ exists, each line item gets a rate — for concrete per m³, brickwork per m², steel per tonne. These rates usually come from a maintained cost database, adjusted for the specific project's location, access, and market conditions at time of tender. This is the method most QS work centres on day to day.

Detailed estimating: building a rate from nothing

When there's no comparable historical rate — an unusual specification, an imported material, a non-standard method — the rate has to be built up analytically:

  • Labour — output rate (units per gang per day) × labour cost per day.
  • Material — quantity required including wastage, at current delivered price.
  • Plant — hire or ownership cost apportioned to the item.
  • Overheads and profit — added as a percentage on top of the direct cost.
Site tip Keep your rate database alive, not archived. A rate from a project 3 years ago is a starting point, not an answer — always adjust for current material prices and the specific site's access and conditions before reusing it.

What separates an accurate estimate from a rough guess

  • A site visit before pricing, not just a desk-based takeoff from drawings.
  • An explicit contingency percentage, sized to how much design information is actually available.
  • Rates adjusted for inflation and current market prices, not pulled unchanged from an old job.
  • An elemental cost breakdown the client can actually read — not just a single lump-sum number.

The goal isn't to eliminate uncertainty — early in a project, that's impossible. The goal is to be precise about how uncertain your number actually is, and to use the right technique for the stage you're estimating at.
